Every tech enthusiast has one; the junk drawer, the tote full of cables, the shelf stacked with long-deprecated laptops. Containers filled with objects that to all sensible laypeople would be considered e-waste but contain the spark of hope in their stewards that maybe, just maybe, that decades-old cable that fits a long deprecated standard will some day have the chance to step in and save the day.
Recently I had the opportunity to do what I like best: step outside the bounds of the typical office-bound IT solutions and take on a challenge that allowed me to flex muscles not used in decades to solve an outsized problem for a client. Industrial manufacturing is an odd beast in the IT world; esoteric solutions that lay parallel to normal IT practices; entire million-dollar operations and hundreds of thousands of dollars worth of machinery laid down at the feet of a wheezy computer old enough to remember sub-$1 gas prices. A feudal empire built up on a foundation of toothpicks for whom any first-party support has long since ceased to exist, replaced by a knowing nod and an expression somewhere between a grimace and a smirk accompanied with the offer of a six-figure contract to rip and replace the whole empire for lack of a new toothpick.
Programmable Logic Controllers, otherwise known as PLCs, are a simple concept. A small embedded computer designed to exist in the harsh environment of a chemical processing plant that is connected to a myriad of sensors, switches, motors, and actuators with instructions on how to make the whole circus function. These come in as many form-factors as there are grains of sand in the universe, but the most common architecture consists of a modular PLC with separate modules to power the system, carry out instruction processing, relay modules that control the machine hardware, sensor modules that collect information for the processor to act on, and the hero of today’s story: the Human Machine Interface.
As with most industrial equipment, a Human Machine Interface is an ostentatious name for “a computer with a screen attached,” and is the way most workers interact with the PLCs. Often this is configured to show the status of various equipment and display what the attached machinery is doing in an easy digestible way, as well as presenting logical onscreen controls that the workers can interact with to control the machine. Truly an important toothpick. So when a client called in with word that their HMI was on the fritz, out of support, and an upgrade from the manufacturer was quoted north of $250,000, I had a fair idea the scope of what we were about to step into.
As a card-carrying nerd with an affinity for the unusual the HMI in question was a thing of beauty; thick gauge sheet metal made up a sealed environment for a computer that, on paper, was obsolete even in its 1997 date of manufacturer, but was built to a degree of durability and quality that was jaw dropping. The Voyager 1 probe was launched in 1977 and remains functional in interstellar space, and I have no doubts that this HMI could accomplish the same were it not for the fault of the solitary moving part in the whole system: the hard drive.
Nowadays so-called Solid-State Drives or SSDs are ubiquitous, but the prior technology effected a CD-like disc spinning constantly and read by an arm that floated over the surface on a cushion of air. A fault in the spindle motor, the arm motor, or a vibration large enough to rattle the read head through that cushion of air into the surface of the disk would spell disaster for the data on it. In this case the issue was the arm motor would intermittently refuse to clock into work. Such failures are not irrecoverable; the client told tale of this happening previously and requiring the services of a forensic data recovery team to retrieve the program from their stricken drive. This replacement drive, manufactured in 2002, was singing its death rattle as the hulking mass of industrial steel it controlled lay dormant and the clients balance sheets began to show less-favorable numbers.
In any crisis the first step to take always lies in the direction of harm mitigation; how can I prevent a bad situation from getting worse? In this case, knowing the expense of data recovery I immediately prioritized getting the data off the drive. Happily, since it had not failed completely I was able to find an old adapter for the drive’s long-obsolete IDE interface to plug it into my laptop and pull the data off twice; once as a high-level directory structure of files and folders, and once as a 1:1 bit-by-bit copy of the entire drive. This second one was risky because it involves moving the drive stepwise through the entirety of its capacity, a stressful operation which can be the final nail in the coffin for a faulty hard drive. Happily, both backup operations were successful and I was even able to verify the disk image by starting up a virtual copy of the HMI computer on my laptop, albeit not a very useful copy due to missing critical hardware components that only existed in the depths of a computer from the Clinton administration.
After a sigh of relief it’s time to take inventory: an esoteric computer more at home in 1992 than 2026, a 24 year old replacement drive gasping its last, and no hope of being able to walk into a store and walk out with anything suitable. I’d have to scrounge. Fortunately, my boss and I are two branches of the same metaphorical tree and I had my pick of hard drives from the early 2000s to choose from. A brief search found a 40 gigabyte Western Digital that seemed at first glance to be suitable, and as I wrote the HMI’s image to it a small voice in the back of my head kept reminding me of something:
Old computers are weird.
The modern concept of “plug and play” didn’t even exist when the software running this machine was created; it was running MS-DOS 6.22, an entirely text-based system whose main claim to fame over previous versions was the forward-looking support for dates beyond 1999. Computers weren’t “figured out” yet, and seemingly esoteric limitations existed simply because nobody had ever considered that perhaps hard drives would grow to be multiple gigabytes, or RAM in excess of 128 Megabytes. An apocryphal quote often attributed to Bill Gates in the 80s purports that “640kb of RAM ought to be enough for anybody,” now in 2026 a baseline office computer generally has 25,000 times that amount. Suffice it to say these restrictions of the olden days came to haunt me as I dug through the trenches of the Internet to find a user manual for the motherboard in this old industrial computer which laid it bare for me: Supports up to 8.4 GB HDD. I’m not great at math, but after a few moments of hard thought I concluded that my 40 GB drive was not going to work. 
As I sat in the drive-thru of a local taco joint eagerly awaiting future gastrointestinal agony I remembered an artifact buried deep in the cobweb filled corners of my basement. There, under a pile of cardboard boxes containing scrapbooks and tchotchkes was a 1995 Toshiba Satellite Pro 400CDT and in it, hopefully, was a solution. A brief fight with Apple Carplay and I had my girlfriend on the phone and was walking through navigating the spider-infested depths to find the laptop, flip it over, remove the battery, and photograph the shockingly accessible hard drive. A 5GB IBM TravelStar.


 
Our hero of the day

The rest of the story is simple; I retrieved the laptop hard drive, paused to consider the less than stellar noises that emanated from it when gently rotated about its axis, and concluded that it was none of my business. Into the drive adapter, written with the trusty backup image from the HMI, and gently carried like a newborn child over to the gaping maw of industrial automation. Some quick configuration in the system setup was necessary—remember, plug-and-play was an unknown luxury in these days—and the machine shuddered to life and began dutifully displaying the status of the apparatus surrounding us. The client made a noise somewhere between astonishment and a sob and began chanting instructions into the two-way radio rubbing its way through his left coverall strap as the company came back to life. 
This is not the story of a fix, but of a band-aid. I have no delusions that a noisy IBM TravelStar is a lasting solution, but it did serve as a second chance for the client to resume operations while engaging a local automation engineer to discuss an upgrade path.
And I got to use my junk drawer.
