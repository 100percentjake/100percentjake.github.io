There has been much ado about Jaguar in the news. And now that the dust has settled and I’ve fully torpedo’d any chance I had of riding the wave of interest on the news cycle I thought I’d take a critical and hopefully measured look at something that is of great personal interest to me: Jaguar Cars.

On December 2nd of 2024 after much speculation Jaguar unveiled the Type 00, a styling concept meant to preview the upcoming 2026 lineup of fresh new reinvented Jaguar EVs. Their advertising campaign invoked haute couture fashion shows and Neomodernist design festooned with catchy two-word maxims like “Copy nothing” and “delete ordinary”.

![[img/jaguar/twojags.jpeg]]

I’m not going to spend much time on this marketing because, frankly, its disingenuousness bores me. Which it is (disingenuous). Arguing to copy nothing while being one of the last at the table in a global adoption of EVs is laughable at best, especially when combined with a new corporate typeface that consists of thin lines and lower-case stylization.

![[img/jaguar/jaguarlogo.jpg]]

The rest of the marketing consists of a diverse cast of characters in outfits that Jaguar did at least have the good graces of tapping British artists in the design of. It’s all very serviceable though I cannot comment on it too much lacking the mindset of the sort of demographic Jaguar seems to be targeting, which I will get into later. And then there’s the car.

Copy nothing? Someone at Jaguar Land Rover copied the design of the new flagship Range Rover and pasted it on the silhouette of a generic 2+2 long-nose coupe, the sort of vehicle that Jaguar is world renowned for. Copy nothing? You’ve copied yourself twice.

## A six figure Jag

Jaguar’s making a very clear move upmarket with this unveiling, which is not unprecedented. Several times in their history Jaguar has made a very stark jump in the pricing of their models on offer; the XJS cost twice what the E-type did, for example. Their goal appears to be to create cars that are bought not for any rational or objective reason, but for purely emotional ones. Which isn’t a terrible plan; cars are already an extremely emotionally driven purchase. They convey your needs, wants, tastes, and in some cases desires to everybody in eyesight between you and your destination. Many companies such as Mansory have made unfathomable amounts of money taking already expensive objects of desire and creating the most unthinkably ugly thing you can imagine out of them because when someone in a $275,000 Lamborghini Urus sees a Mansory Urus cruise by they just got upstaged by a car that costs 8 times as much.

![[img/jaguar/mansory.jpg]]

And this market, unfortunately, is much larger than one might expect. According to Fox News the top 1% in America, consisting of about 3.3 million people, average an annual income of $800,000 and early speculation says that Jaguar’s new lineup will cost in the $127,000 range, which gives them a potential buying pool of at least that 3.3 million individuals. That buying pool is also the target of progressively insane extremely limited run supercars and hypercars like the Lamborghini Sian at $3.7 million or the older Sesto Elemento for $2.92 million.

For the demographic spending the GDP of a small country on barely-usable limited run hypercars things like practicality, range, and even beauty rarely factor into the equation. A mere $127k for a snazzy and blatantly compromised EV with novelty and heritage? You might pick one up for your 4th mistress and her husband to use on the weekends. It would sure stand out compared to a Taycan, 8 series, or the now-ancient Lexus LC.

![[img/jaguar/type00blue.jpeg]]

As far as the car itself, I do appreciate many aspects about it. Embracing the flexibility given to a designer with an EV skateboard design to make the most absurd long-hood villainous penis car imaginable is completely absurd and I love it. But it’s hard to talk too much about the design of the Type 00 because of what I believe to be the worst part of this entire escapade and the thing that I believe is Jaguar thoroughly shooting themselves in the foot and wasting the excellent momentum that they had going. But I have to talk about Steve Jobs for a moment.

## Product Releases

![[img/jaguar/youngjobs.jpg]]

Apple in the 90s was in a very similar position that Jaguar is in currently. They had a rapidly aging lineup of machines barely distinguishable with their competition and no clear path forwards until Steve Jobs rejoined the company, threw out every single product that was being produced and replaced them all with a product lineup that not only made their current market offerings appeal more to consumers like the colorful iMac G3, but created entirely new market segments seemingly out of thin air by taking existing burgeoning concepts like MP3 players and cell phones and applying a singular design vision to them.

I attribute much of Job’s success to his disdain for his own customers. He relied on his own vision and his iron-fisted control over a team of superstar designers and engineers to create what he sincerely believed was the best product that he could make. No focus groups, no market research.

And, importantly, the keynotes.

![[img/jaguar/jobskeynote.jpg]]

Apple product unveilings used to be borderline magical, busting onto the scene with some new product you could have barely imagined taking a simple concept like “laptop” and condensing it down and refining that concept until you can make an entire audience gasp as you pull a laptop out of a manilla envelope. Apple likes to use the word “revolutionary” but I’d instead classify it as incredibly well measured and executed evolution.

And then, critically, after being suddenly bowled over by the newest supposed innovation from the fruit company you could, within 24 hours, spend your money on one. No teases, no “coming soon”, no concepts. Here is our new product, you may now buy it.

Jaguar did not do this. Jaguar didn’t even do “coming soon”. Jaguar made something they perceived as bold, set it on the table, said “you can’t have this” and refused to elaborate further.

Aside from simply wasting any momentum that could have been converted into sales or even pre-orders and money into Jaguars’ pocket, we got a design concept and a “stay tuned”.

If anybody in Coventry had an ounce of sense they would have, at the bare minimum, had one production-intent vehicle ready to show people exactly what they can expect to have in their garage in 2026 with pre-orders. Ideally it would be a fully-realized lineup of EV sedan, coupe, and open topped roadster to stand alongside Land Rover’s lineup of gas SUVs as a complimentary flipside of the same coin.

## Heritage

![[img/jaguar/xj12.jpg]]

The reason I commend Steve Jobs for ignoring the opinions of the greater buying public is because, more often than not, the loudest voices are the ones that were never going to buy your product anyway. This is a philosophy that I mourn the death of in modern times of design by committee and focus groups generating seventeen variations of the same slop delivered to us end over end. Coupe SUVs? The same as a normal SUV but with utterly wrecked rear headroom and cargo space? Because some dullard in a leased base C class said he didn’t want to upgrade to a GLK because it just didn’t look enough like an AMG GT?

![[img/jaguar/x6m.webp]]

Likewise on the internet you get seas of owners of $4,000 1994 XJ6s and rapidly disintegrating XK8 convertibles spitting out their cup of hot brown and angrily proclaiming that Jaguar has lost a customer! It’s over! They’ve thrown their heritage in the garbage and will regret this decision dearly! You can’t change what makes Jaguar Jaguar like this! How could they replace the smooth, sleek Jaguar F-Type coupe with this bulky, bold, slab sided abomination?


When your average person thinks of Jaguar they probably come up blank because, honestly, what normal person off the street could name a single Jaguar model? When a car enthusiast thinks of Jaguar, they likely think of the E-Type.

![[img/jaguar/etype.webp]]

Beloved for its timeless styling, sporting capability, and penis proportions, the Jaguar E-Type has long been a symbol of Jaguar and Britain in general. Lithe in appearance and nimble in stature the E-Type sold 72,000 units in its 13 years of production. In 1975 Jaguar was on the verge of being killed off as a brand after British Leyland Motor Corporation’s restructuring midway through the year. Their only hope was the XJ27 project, the last project that aerodynamics engineer Malcolm Sayer and Jaguar founder Sir William Lyons would ever work on.

After 7 years in development the successor to the then-dated Jaguar E-Type was unveiled in September of 1975 as the Jaguar XJ-S and the refrain was retrospectively familiar.

“How could they replace the smooth, sleek Jaguar E-Type with this bulky, bold, slab sided abomination?”

The design, driven by more modern understandings of aerodynamics by Malcolm Sayer but with a desire to keep the DNA of Jaguar from Sir William, received immediate ire. In the context of its predecessor the XJ-S was a dramatic departure from form. Larger, heavier, and far more expensive owing to Jaguar’s conglomerate ownership demanding it be placed in a more expensive market segment.

![[img/jaguar/xjsyellow.jpeg]]

Larger, heavier, more expensive, dramatic styling, widely negative public engagement. Sound familiar?

And yet, by the end of its run, the XJS had seen two design evolutions each one out-selling the previous version unlike the E-Type which sold strong and fast and quickly tapered off. By the end of its 21 year run the XJS had handily outsold the E-type at 115,413 cars produced, and formed the literal foundation for the subsequent Ford-owned era of Jaguar as its chassis and suspension was used as the basis for the XK8 and Aston Martin DB7 that brought Jaguar into the 2000s where it languished for some time as a somewhat confused subdivision of Ford with two models of their own, the XK8 and XJ which were both rebodies of their predecessors, and two models that were rebodies of front wheel drive Fords. Despite their underwhelming reality the Ford-based models sold extremely well for Jaguar, with the S-Type moving 150k units between 1999 and 2009 and the X-type moving 200,000.

To my knowledge this is the only time Jaguar has ever made a move down-market, and it didn’t work. Despite the rousing sales numbers the Ford-owned Jaguar never made a profit and was eventually spun off along with Land Rover to Tata Motors of India for 1.7 billion pounds as a wholly-owned British subsidiary in June 2008.

## The modern era

![[img/jaguar/jagxj.jpeg]]

The second generation of the Ian Callum-designed XK grand tourer was the only Jaguar model to survive the transition with all other Jaguar models either immediately or shortly after ending production. The S-type, X-type, and XJ8 all possessed throwback retro styling harkening back to the Jaguar of the late 60s with quad headlights and traditional 3-box design, and all were thrown out. In came the new Jaguar XJ, another Callum design that had begun development before the Tata acquisition and possessed a sleeker, longer, smoother fastback-esque aesthetic. The S-type gave way to the XF, built on an evolution of the same platform but with the same Ian Callum styling. Much later, the X-type was replaced with the XE as a front engined rear wheel drive 3-series competitor that, critically, was the first Jaguar under Tata Motors to be on the new D7 platform that would allow the model line to be expanded and model R&D consolidated.

With this new platform in their back pocket Tata pulled the XF onto the same platform with the XE in 2015 and then put it on stilts to create the brand’s first SUV: the F-Pace. This would later be joined by a rebadged Range Rover Evoque called the E-Pace and the brand’s first electric car in 2018: The I-Pace.

![[img/jaguar/ipace.jpeg]]

And then nothing.

![[img/jaguar/jaguar-lineup-2020.jpg]]

A flurry of activity, restructuring, platform consolidation, and the production of gorgeous and dramatic Ian Callum designs of all sizes just suddenly stopped after 2018 and the model line became completely stagnant.

Mirroring the moves of Porsche and BMW over a decade earlier the F-Pace was far and away Jaguar’s best selling model peaking at 72,000 sold in 2018 and totaling 343,000 total by 2025 making it Jaguar’s second best selling model to the XF, which had an 8 year head start on the F pace and managed to sell 462,000 by 2025.

But… that was it. Outside of their large SUV and large sedan Jaguar languished in a malaise of minimal development and even more minimal product reliability. The much celebrated among enthusiasts F-Type managed 87,000 sold units in 13 years and their forward looking I-Pace struggled along at barely 15,000 units per year failing to compete with the similarly priced Tesla Model X before sales plummeted to nothing after the release of the Model Y.

But Jaguar doesn’t operate alone. It’s a part of Jaguar Land Rover and business for the Land Rover portion was booming, with a 54% sales increase in 2019 attributed largely to the second generation Evoque, and company stock price climbing nearly 300% between 2020 and 2025 or up 2,200% since 2009.

This leaves Jaguar in a bind. A wart, as it were, consisting of stale models existing alongside a vastly more successful brand. History has shown that moving Jaguar down-market would be a mistake, and Range Rover will sell you a luxurious box on 20-something inch wheels at every price point between 49 and 107 thousand dollars. So what are the corporate overlords at Tata to do?

Reinvent Jaguar. Just like before.

![[img/jaguar/deleteordinary.webp]]

From a business perspective Jaguar as an EV brand provides Tata with the ability to share and further develop their Ziptron brand of EV technologies, and the resulting carbon credits can allow Land Rover to produce 600hp fire breathing Range Rovers with aplomb. This is a strategy that General Motors has employed with their portfolio of EV crossovers allowing continued development of V8 gas trucks and the Corvette with more agility in the face of uncertain environmental regulations.

From a branding perspective Jaguar as a ritzy EV brand provides prospective Range Rover buyers with more of a tech-bro bent a potentially compelling option. Or it would, if any cars actually existed outside of fairy tale styling concepts.

## Conclusion

Jaguar’s current situation is the incredibly interesting result of a thriving multinational conglomerate choosing to run buckwild with an 80 year old brand instead of shutting it down and letting the brand name be passed around endless startups, holding companies, and venture capital vultures before ending up as DeLorean has spent the last decades. Hawked as a nameplate on a CAD model on Kickstarter.

I remain cautiously optimistic. In the vacuum of Jaguar the brand wanting to move up-market I would have positioned them as a Pininfarina style design house building small run or bespoke ultra luxury experiences on top of existing EV platforms from other manufacturers such as GM’s Ultium, much like Rolls Royce’s Bespoke program.

But in the greater reality of Jaguar being a part of Jaguar-LandRover that would be a completely insane waste of the potential to share the costs and benefits of R&D with Land Rover.

![[img/jaguar/rrlineup.jpg]]

As for me, I remain curious and cautiously optimistic. Blustering about Jaguar throwing away their heritage is simply showing ignorance with the tumultuous history of Jaguar as a brand, especially when the Type 00 has such an incredibly close parallel with the Jaguar XJS.
